Listening Part 4

Listening to a News Item

Follow a news report and identify the main event, important facts, background information, causes, effects, and updates.

Section 1 — What Is This Part? Official format: 5 questions SelfPIP study guide
Golden rule: Find the main event first, then connect the supporting facts.

What is this part?

In Part 4 you listen to a news report about a single event: what happened, who was involved, why it happened and what happens next.

The task tests whether you can separate the main event from the supporting facts, follow cause and effect, and keep track of any update.

In everyday terms: you hear a short news story and answer questions about what happened and why it matters.

Official format vs SelfPIP advice: Part 4 officially contains 5 questions. The focus areas, strategies, signals, traps and examples on this page are SelfPIP study guidance — they are not official CELPIP rules.

What Should I Expect?

General expectations

Part 4 is a short news report rather than a conversation. The audio is not published in advance, so treat the points below as general expectations.

  • A report about one event. The story stays with a single main event instead of covering several.
  • The main event comes early. News reports usually state the headline information in the first sentence or two.
  • Supporting facts follow. Background, numbers, names and quotations fill in the details afterwards.
  • Cause and effect. The report usually explains why something happened and what the consequences are.
  • Developments or updates. A later line may add to or adjust the information ("officials now say...").
  • Attribution matters. Part of the report is the reporter's summary and part is what a person or organisation said.

How a news report usually develops

Main event stated
↓
Key facts (who, what, where, when)
↓
Background
↓
Cause and effect
↓
What happens next
How to prepare: practise identifying the first sentence of a news story — that sentence normally carries the main event.

What Should I Listen For?

SelfPIP focus points

Keep these six targets in mind while the report plays. They cover what Part 4 questions usually ask about.

A

The main event

What actually happened?

This is usually stated in the opening sentences.

B

Who, what, where, when

The basic facts of the story

The classic news questions — all five may appear in the questions.

C

Cause

Why did it happen?

Reports usually explain the reason behind the event.

D

Effect

What is the consequence?

Look for what changes for the people involved.

E

Background

What came before?

Background explains why the event matters now.

F

Updates

What happens next, and has anything changed?

A late line can adjust what you heard earlier.

Question-Type Strategy

SelfPIP guidance

Part 4 questions usually test one of these kinds of information. Expand a card to see how to handle it.

What it may ask

"What is the main event in this report?"

How to approach it

Choose the central event, not one supporting fact or one person's quote.

What it may ask

"When will the change take effect?" / "Who is affected?"

How to approach it

Attach the detail to the right part of the story as you note it.

What it may ask

"Why is the route changing?"

How to approach it

Listen for "because", "due to" and "the decision comes after".

What it may ask

"What is the result of the decision?"

How to approach it

Look for "as a result", "which means" and consequences for residents or travellers.

What it may ask

"What did officials say later in the report?"

How to approach it

Stay with the report to the end and mark any correction or new information.

Remember: these are common question purposes, not guaranteed question wording, and a question type never guarantees an easy or difficult item.

Step-by-Step Strategy

SelfPIP guidance

Use the same three phases every time. In this part the opening sentences matter most.

1

Before listening

Decide what the question is asking about the story.

  • Ask yourself whether the question wants what happened, why, or what happens next.
  • Expect the first sentence to carry the main event.
2

During listening

Follow the story from the main event to the details.

  • Catch the headline sentence and hold on to it.
  • Note who, what, where and when as they appear.
  • Link cause to effect with an arrow, and mark any update.
3

After the relevant information

Compare the choices and choose the supported option.

  • If the question asks about the story as a whole, prefer the main event over a detail.
  • Reject options that invent information nobody stated.

The four moves to remember

Catch the main event
↓
Note who, what, where, when
↓
Link cause to effect
↓
Mark any update

Common Traps

SelfPIP guidance

⚠ Trap 1 — Choosing a supporting detail as the main event

How it feels"The report mentioned a temporary bus stop, so that is the main event."

InsteadAsk what the whole report is about; details like stops and numbers support the main event.

⚠ Trap 2 — Confusing cause with effect

How it feels"The bridge is closed, so the closure must be the cause of the repair work."

InsteadCheck the direction: the repair work causes the closure, and the closure causes the route change.

⚠ Trap 3 — Mixing up who said what

How it feels"The reporter said it, so it must be a fact."

InsteadNotice the attribution: some lines are the reporter's summary and some are a quotation.

⚠ Trap 4 — Missing an update that changes the story

How it feels"The first version of the story is the answer."

InsteadStay to the end — a later line can add or adjust information.

⚠ Trap 5 — Overthinking beyond the report

How it feels"There is probably a reason they did not mention, so I will guess."

InsteadAnswer with what the report supports; do not import outside knowledge.

Remember: these traps are SelfPIP study guidance, not official rules. They are best used as a checklist of your own habits when you review your practice.

Useful Signals

Recognition only

Main event

Language that states the story in one line.

  • "officials say..."
  • "the city has announced..."
  • "residents in the area are..."

Background

Language that explains what came before.

  • "this follows..."
  • "the decision comes after..."
  • "earlier this year..."

Cause

Language that gives the reason.

  • "because of..."
  • "the delay was caused by..."
  • "due to the repair work..."

Effect

Language that gives the consequence.

  • "as a result..."
  • "which means..."
  • "passengers will need to..."

Update

Language that adds or corrects information.

  • "however, authorities now say..."
  • "in an update this morning..."
  • "it now appears that..."
These expressions are examples of signals that often carry important information. They do not always mark the answer: the meaning of the whole turn decides that, not one single word.

Note-Taking Strategy

Small and fast

Do not write sentences. Capture the main event first, then the facts that support it, and mark any change.

  • P problem / plan
  • S solution / option
  • * agreement
  • − disagreement or rejected
  • → result or change
  • ? unclear

What a Part 4 note might look like

Main: route 12 → Bridge Rd (from 3 Mar) Why: Mill St bridge repairs How long: ~6 weeks Also: extra buses at peak Quote: spokesperson → +5 min ? exact timetable

Rules that keep notes useful:
  • One or two words per idea — never a full sentence.
  • Always mark agreement and rejection: that is usually where the answer changes.
  • If you miss something, write "?" and keep going.
  • Use the same symbols every time you practise so they stay automatic.

Original Mini Example

Original practice

This is an original SelfPIP example, not a CELPIP recording. Read the short news report, then answer the question — nothing is revealed until you choose.

Situation A local radio bulletin reports a temporary change to a city bus route while a bridge is repaired.
You hear "Bus route twelve will change next month. From the third of March, the route will follow Bridge Road instead of Mill Street while the Mill Street bridge is repaired."
You hear "The work is expected to last about six weeks, and extra buses will run at peak times while it continues."
You hear "A transit spokesperson said the change should add roughly five minutes to most journeys."
You hear "Passengers who usually board on Mill Street can use the temporary stop at the corner of Bridge Road and Second Avenue."

Question — Why is route 12 changing?

What to notice: the main event and its cause appeared in the first two sentences, while the figures and the quotation came later as supporting detail.

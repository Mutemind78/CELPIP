Listening Part 6

Listening to Viewpoints

Understand different perspectives, supporting reasons, examples, comparisons, and the difference between facts and opinions.

Section 1 — What Is This Part? Official format: 6 questions SelfPIP study guide
Golden rule: Identify the viewpoint → find its support → compare the ideas.

What is this part?

In Part 6 you listen to one or more speakers expressing a viewpoint: an opinion about a topic, usually with reasons, examples and comparisons.

The task tests whether you can identify each viewpoint, see how it is supported, separate facts from opinions, and understand meaning that is implied rather than stated directly.

In everyday terms: you listen to someone explain what they think and why, and then answer questions about their position and their reasoning.

Official format vs SelfPIP advice: Part 6 officially contains 6 questions. The focus areas, strategies, signals, traps and examples on this page are SelfPIP study guidance — they are not official CELPIP rules.

What Should I Expect?

General expectations

Part 6 is opinion-based speech rather than factual reporting. The audio is not published in advance, so treat the points below as general expectations.

  • Viewpoints, not just facts. The speaker is expressing what they believe, recommend or criticise.
  • Support follows the view. Reasons, examples and comparisons usually come straight after the opinion.
  • Facts and opinions mix. A speaker may use a fact — a figure or a report — to support an opinion.
  • Some meaning is implied. Doubts, suggestions and criticism can be expressed indirectly.
  • Hedges appear. Words like "tends to", "often" and "in most cases" limit how strong the claim is.
  • Two perspectives may be compared. The speaker may describe an opposing view before explaining their own.

How a viewpoint usually develops

Topic introduced
↓
Viewpoint stated
↓
Reasons given
↓
Examples or comparisons
↓
Viewpoint restated or summarised
How to prepare: practise separating what a speaker believes from what is simply true — mark facts with an "F" and opinions with an "O".

What Should I Listen For?

SelfPIP focus points

Keep these six targets in mind while the extract plays. They cover what Part 6 questions usually ask about.

A

The viewpoint

What does the speaker believe?

Find the position first; the details only make sense around it.

B

Supporting reasons

Why do they believe it?

Reasons usually follow the opinion immediately.

C

Examples

What evidence or illustration is given?

Examples often answer questions about why something is mentioned.

D

Fact or opinion

Is this stated or believed?

Separate what the speaker reports from what they evaluate.

E

Comparisons

What are the advantages and disadvantages?

Speakers often weigh two options before endorsing one.

F

Implied meaning

What is suggested but not stated?

Watch for hedges and indirect criticism.

Question-Type Strategy

SelfPIP guidance

Part 6 questions usually test one of these kinds of information. Expand a card to see how to handle it.

What it may ask

"What is the speaker's opinion about...?"

How to approach it

Match the option to the position, including any limitation the speaker added.

What it may ask

"Why does the speaker support...?"

How to approach it

Connect the reason to the viewpoint rather than to the topic in general.

What it may ask

"Why does the speaker mention...?"

How to approach it

Ask what the example is doing: supporting a reason, or introducing a contrast.

What it may ask

"Which statement is a fact?" / "Which is presented as an opinion?"

How to approach it

Check for evidence language: reported figures and studies are facts; judgements are opinions.

What it may ask

"What does the speaker imply?"

How to approach it

Use what was said plus what it reasonably suggests — and do not go further.

Remember: these are common question purposes, not guaranteed question wording, and a question type never guarantees an easy or difficult item.

Step-by-Step Strategy

SelfPIP guidance

Use the same three phases every time. In this part, separating facts from opinions is what keeps the answer accurate.

1

Before listening

Identify the topic and expect viewpoints.

  • Ask yourself what the extract is about and who is expressing a view.
  • Expect questions about the speaker's position, reasoning or implications.
2

During listening

Track each viewpoint with its support.

  • Write the viewpoint as V1 or V2, with a one-word tag.
  • Mark support: F for facts, O for opinions.
  • Note hedges, because they limit the strength of a claim.
3

After the relevant information

Compare the choices and choose the supported option.

  • Check whether the question asks what was stated or what is implied.
  • Reject options that are stronger or broader than the speaker's claim.

The four moves to remember

Catch the viewpoint
↓
Mark its support (F = fact, O = opinion)
↓
Compare the ideas
↓
Watch for hedges and implied meaning
"Identify the viewpoint → find its support → compare the ideas."

Common Traps

SelfPIP guidance

⚠ Trap 1 — Treating an opinion as a fact

How it feels"The speaker said borrowing is up, so everyone agrees the hours are worth it."

InsteadBorrowing figures are facts; "worth keeping" is the speaker's opinion. Keep the two apart.

⚠ Trap 2 — Ignoring hedges and overstating the view

How it feels"The speaker thinks the change should be permanent."

InsteadNotice limiting words — "in most cases", "for the next year or two" — and choose the option that matches that strength.

⚠ Trap 3 — Attributing a described view to the speaker

How it feels"The speaker mentioned the budget concerns, so the speaker shares them."

InsteadCheck who holds the view: a speaker often describes an opposing position before answering it.

⚠ Trap 4 — Inferring beyond what was said

How it feels"They did not say it, but the implication is obvious."

InsteadStay with what is stated or reasonably implied; do not build a longer chain of reasoning.

⚠ Trap 5 — Matching words instead of meaning

How it feels"The option repeats 'cost', and cost was mentioned."

InsteadAsk whether the option matches the speaker's position on that point.

Remember: these traps are SelfPIP study guidance, not official rules. They are best used as a checklist of your own habits when you review your practice.

Useful Signals

Recognition only

Viewpoint

Language that states a position or belief.

  • "I believe..."
  • "to my mind..."
  • "it seems to me that..."

Support

Language that gives a reason for the view.

  • "this is because..."
  • "one reason is..."
  • "the evidence suggests..."

Fact marker

Language that reports rather than judges.

  • "according to the report..."
  • "the figures show..."
  • "borrowing went up by..."

Contrast

Language that introduces the other side.

  • "however..."
  • "on the other hand..."
  • "while that may be true, ..."

Hedge

Language that limits how strong a claim is.

  • "tends to..."
  • "in most cases..."
  • "arguably..."
These expressions are examples of signals that often carry important information. They do not always mark the answer: the meaning of the whole turn decides that, not one single word.
8

Note-Taking Strategy

Small and fast

Do not write sentences. Keep the viewpoint and its support side by side, and mark facts separately from opinions.

  • P problem / plan
  • S solution / option
  • * agreement
  • − disagreement or rejected
  • → result or change
  • ? unclear

What a Part 6 note might look like

Topic: library weekend hours V: keep the longer hours (O) Support: weekend borrowing ↑ (F); students need study space; volunteers offered hours − cost: staff hours up ... donations covered most (F) → 'worth keeping' — hedged, next 1–2 yrs

Rules that keep notes useful:
  • One or two words per idea — never a full sentence.
  • Always mark agreement and rejection: that is usually where the answer changes.
  • If you miss something, write "?" and keep going.
  • Use the same symbols every time you practise so they stay automatic.

Original Mini Example

Original practice

This is an original SelfPIP example, not a CELPIP recording. Read the short commentary, then answer the question — nothing is revealed until you choose.

Situation A speaker at a public meeting gives their view about the longer weekend opening hours at the local library, including the reasons and the financial concern.
You hear "I want to explain why I think our library should keep its longer weekend hours."
You hear "Two things convinced me. First, borrowing goes up every weekend — Saturday is now our busiest day. Second, students tell us they have nowhere quiet to study when the campus library closes."
You hear "There is a cost, of course: staff hours are higher than they used to be. But donations from the friends-of-the-library group covered most of that last year."
You hear "So while I understand the budget concerns, I believe the longer hours are worth keeping — certainly for the next year or two."

Question — What is the speaker's viewpoint about the longer weekend hours?

What to notice: the speaker mixed facts (borrowing figures and donations) with an opinion ("worth keeping"). The correct option keeps the position and the limitation the speaker added.

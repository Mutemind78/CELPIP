CELPIP Speaking · Task 1

Giving Advice

Give practical advice for a situation while showing empathy, explaining your recommendations, and supporting them with reasons.

30 s preparation 90 s response SelfPIP study guide
Golden rule: Acknowledge the situation first, then give one clear recommendation — backed by a reason and a short example.
1 What this task is

What Is This Task?

In Task 1 a prompt describes a person — a friend, a relative, a coworker — facing a problem or a decision. You speak directly to that person and give them practical advice, as if you were having a real conversation.

Your advice has to be genuinely useful: show that you understand where they are, recommend one clear course of action, and explain why it would help. When you finish, the listener should know exactly what you suggest they do.

Preparation

30 seconds — read the situation, pick ONE main piece of advice.

Response

90 seconds — the longest response time in the test. Plan to fill it.

You are speaking

to one person about one situation — keep it personal and direct.

Official format vs SelfPIP advice: The task description and timings above are official CELPIP format information. The CARE framework, language banks, traps, practice prompt, and model response on this page are SelfPIP study guidance — not official CELPIP material, and not a scoring rule.
2 How to answer

How to Answer This Task

The hub's CARE framework fits this task exactly. Say the four parts in order, and the 90 seconds organize themselves:

C — Context → A — Advice → R — Reason → E — Example
  1. Preparation (30 s): Read the situation twice at most. Decide ONE recommendation and the strongest reason for it — resist listing three half-recommendations.
  2. Context (≈10 s): Show you understood their situation in one sentence: "I can see that you're torn about whether to..."
  3. Advice (≈15 s): Give your recommendation directly: "I'd strongly suggest that you..." One main piece of advice only.
  4. Reason (≈30 s): Explain WHY it helps — this is where most of the 90 seconds should go. Develop the reason fully instead of stacking new ones.
  5. Example (≈25 s): Ground it: a quick story of when the advice worked (yours or someone you know), or what would happen in their specific case.
  6. Close (≈10 s): One warm, confident line — "Whatever you decide, I'm sure it'll work out" — so the response doesn't just run out of air.
Timing reality: 90 seconds is long. If you finish your example early, add a second reason or extend the example — silence or restarting your introduction both cost you.
3 Useful language

Useful Language

Opening with empathy (Context)
  • I can see that you're in a difficult spot with...
  • That sounds really stressful — I've been in a similar situation.
  • I understand why you'd be worried about...
Giving the advice directly (Advice)
  • I'd strongly suggest that you...
  • If I were you, I would...
  • The best thing to do, in my view, is...
  • My main recommendation would be to...
Explaining why (Reason)
  • The main reason is that...
  • That way, you'd avoid... and you'd also get to...
  • What makes this work is...
  • Otherwise, you risk...
Example and closing (Example)
  • For example, my cousin had the same problem and...
  • To give you a concrete case...
  • Take your situation: if you..., then...
  • I'm confident this will help — let me know how it goes.
Stress these words

your verb of advice, the reason's key noun, the example's outcome — let the rest run light.

Signpost cheaply

"First..., The reason..., For example..." — three signposts are plenty in 90 seconds.

4 Common traps

Common Traps

⚠ Trap 1 — Advising before acknowledging

How it feels: "You should study medicine. It's stable." — cold, like you didn't listen to their actual worry.

Instead: One sentence of recognition first ("I know you're worried about job security..."), THEN the recommendation.

⚠ Trap 2 — Three pieces of shallow advice

How it feels: "You could study medicine, or law, or take a gap year..." — a menu, not advice; nothing gets developed.

Instead: Commit to ONE recommendation in the first 15 seconds and spend the remaining 75 seconds developing it.

⚠ Trap 3 — Reasons without an example

How it feels: Abstract justifications ("it will help your career long-term") with no picture attached — thin and generic.

Instead: Follow every reason with proof: a mini-story, a person you know, or their specific case walked through concretely.

⚠ Trap 4 — Stopping at 60 seconds' worth

How it feels: You finish a compact answer around the 60-second mark, then trail off — 30 unused seconds of response time.

Instead: Build the example to fit 90. If you finish early, extend the story or add a second supporting reason — never restart.

5 Practice

Original Practice Prompt

Original SelfPIP prompt — not an official CELPIP question

Situation: Your neighbour mentions that her son, who starts university in two months, has never had to manage money. He spends his entire allowance in the first week and then borrows from his friends. She asks what you would advise him to do.

Task: Give your neighbour's son practical advice on managing his money. Explain why your advice would help, and support it with an example. (30 s preparation → 90 s response)

How to use this prompt: Record yourself in one take. Before you listen back, tick the CARE beats off in your head — did you acknowledge, advise one thing, give a reason, and tell a quick story?
6 Model response

Annotated Model Response

"I can see you're torn because you love both the stability of accounting and the pull of art — that's a genuinely hard call, and it's smart that you're thinking it through before signing anything.

My honest advice would be to take the place on the art program now, while you have no mortgage and no commitments. The main reason is that a one-year break won't derail your accounting path at all — graduate programs and junior roles will still be open when you come back — but a creative year gets harder to justify every year that passes. Once you're deep into a career, taking twelve months off starts to look risky, not adventurous.

Take my cousin, for example: she trained as an accountant at twenty-three and always said she'd 'do art later.' She's forty now, and later never happened. You're in the opposite position — the window is wide open right now. So I'd go: art program this year, accounting next. You really can't lose either way."

Why this works

ContextOpens by mirroring HER exact tension in one sentence — acknowledgement before advice, exactly as CARE demands.
Advice"My honest advice would be to take the place on" — one clear, committed recommendation in the second sentence. No menu of options.
ReasonDevelops WHY for ~35 seconds: reversible risk vs. closing window. This is where the 90 seconds get filled — not in the intro.
ExampleThe cousin story grounds the abstract reason in a person, then ties it back to her situation ("the opposite position").
Close"You really can't lose either way" — a warm confident landing instead of running out of air at 90 seconds.

Mindset

The examiner isn't judging your advice — they're judging how clearly you deliver it. Pick advice you'd actually give, say it like a friend would, and let CARE carry the structure.

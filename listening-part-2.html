Listening Part 2

Listening to a Daily Life Conversation

Understand everyday conversations involving plans, requests, arrangements, changes, and personal situations.

Section 1 — What Is This Part? Official format: 5 questions SelfPIP study guide
Golden rule: Understand the situation first, then track what changes.

What is this part?

In Part 2 you listen to a short, everyday conversation: two people making a plan, arranging something, asking for something, or explaining a personal situation.

The task tests whether you understand the situation and the practical information in it: who is speaking and why, what they agree to do, and — very often — what changes during the conversation.

In everyday terms: you are overhearing a normal chat between people who know each other, and then answering questions about the practical details.

Official format vs SelfPIP advice: Part 2 officially contains 5 questions. The focus areas, strategies, signals, traps and examples on this page are SelfPIP study guidance — they are not official CELPIP rules.

What Should I Expect?

General expectations

Part 2 is a single, short conversation about everyday life. The audio is not published in advance, so treat the points below as general expectations, not a script.

  • Short and informal. The conversation is brief and uses contractions, short turns and everyday vocabulary.
  • Speakers who know each other. Friends, family members, colleagues, or people in a service situation.
  • Practical topics. Plans, bookings, requests, arrangements, changes and personal situations.
  • Tone carries meaning. Friendly, apologetic, hesitant or polite language can change what the speakers actually decide.
  • The situation is clear early. You normally know who is speaking and why within the first few turns.
  • A change is common. One detail — a time, a place or a plan — is often adjusted during the conversation.

The usual shape of a Part 2 conversation

Situation
↓
Plan or request
↓
Response
↓

How to prepare: predict the practical categories — times, places, people and reasons — so you are ready to catch them the moment they appear.

What Should I Listen For?

SelfPIP focus points

Keep these six targets in mind while the conversation plays. They cover what Part 2 questions usually ask about.

A

The situation

Who is speaking, and where are they?

Identify the relationship and the setting early.

B

The purpose

Why are they talking?

A request, a plan, an apology or an arrangement — catch it in the first turns.

C

Plans and arrangements

What, where and when?

Note the practical details: day, time, place and who does what.

D

Changes

What gets adjusted?

A time or place that changes is often exactly what the question asks about.

E

Requests and responses

What is asked, and how is it answered?

A polite request can be accepted, declined or negotiated.

F

Reasons and tone

Why, and how does the speaker sound?

Reasons explain the change; tone shows whether the answer is positive.

Question-Type Strategy

SelfPIP guidance

Part 2 questions usually test one of these kinds of information. Expand a card to see how to handle it.

What it may ask

"Where does this conversation take place?" / "What is the relationship between the speakers?"

How to approach it

Use the first few turns to fix the setting and the relationship before the details arrive.

What it may ask

"What time do they agree on?" / "Where will they meet?"

How to approach it

Note the detail as you hear it: exact times and places matter more than general impressions.

What it may ask

"What do they plan to do?"

How to approach it

Track who arranges what — the speaker who makes the booking usually owns the detail.

What it may ask

"What change do they make?" / "Why is the plan changed?"

How to approach it

Mark any change with an arrow and connect it to its reason.

What it may ask

"What is the purpose of the call?" / "How does the speaker feel?"

How to approach it

Polite or hesitant language usually signals a request or a small problem, not agreement.

Remember: these are common question purposes, not guaranteed question wording, and a question type never guarantees an easy or difficult item.

Step-by-Step Strategy

SelfPIP guidance

Use the same three phases every time. Only the middle phase changes with this part.

1

Before listening

Picture the situation before the details arrive.

  • Read the question and predict the category: a time, a place, a person or a reason.
  • Expect a small change — it is the most common feature of this task.
2

During listening

Track the practical information as it appears.

  • Fix who is speaking and why in the first few turns.
  • Note the plan (time, place, who) as soon as you hear it.
  • Mark any change with an arrow, and note who suggested it.
3

After the relevant information

Compare the choices and choose the supported option.

  • Check each option against the final arrangement, not the first version.
  • Prefer the option that matches the meaning, not the one that repeats a word.

The four moves to remember

Catch the situation
↓
Track the plan
↓
Mark any change
↓
Confirm the final arrangement
"Understand the situation first, then track what changes."

Common Traps

SelfPIP guidance

⚠ Trap 1 — Matching words instead of meaning

How it feels"I heard the word Saturday, so this option must be right."

InsteadAsk what the option actually claims, then check it against the final arrangement.

⚠ Trap 2 — Missing the change of plan

How it feels"They agreed on seven, so the answer is seven."

InsteadListen to the end: a later turn often adjusts the time, the place or the whole plan.

⚠ Trap 3 — Mixing up the speakers

How it feels"Someone wants to postpone — it is probably the person who called."

InsteadTrack who says what. The question usually asks about one specific speaker.

⚠ Trap 4 — Reading a polite hedge as agreement

How it feels"They said they were not sure it works, so they accepted."

InsteadHedged language is doubt, not agreement. Look for a clear acceptance such as "That works for me".

⚠ Trap 5 — Getting stuck or overthinking

How it feels"I missed one detail, so the rest of the conversation is lost."

InsteadWrite "?" and keep listening. One missed detail rarely decides every question.

Remember: these traps are SelfPIP study guidance, not official rules. They are best used as a checklist of your own habits when you review your practice.

Useful Signals

Recognition only

Plan / arrangement

Language that sets up a practical arrangement.

  • "are you free on...?"
  • "what time works for you?"
  • "let's meet at..."

Request

Language that asks for something politely.

  • "could you...?"
  • "would you mind...?"
  • "is it okay if I...?"

Change

Language that adjusts the plan.

  • "actually, something has come up"
  • "can we make it later?"
  • "I'll have to..."

Agreement

Language that confirms the arrangement.

  • "that works for me"
  • "sounds good, see you then"
  • "no problem at all"

Reason

Language that explains the change or the request.

  • "the thing is..."
  • "because..."
  • "that's why I asked"
These expressions are examples of signals that often carry important information. They do not always mark the answer: the meaning of the whole turn decides that, not one single word.

Note-Taking Strategy

Small and fast

Do not write sentences. Capture the practical facts — who, what, when, where — and mark any change.

  • P problem / plan
  • S solution / option
  • * agreement
  • − disagreement or rejected
  • → result or change
  • ? unclear

What a Part 2 note might look like

Plan: dinner Sat 7:00 → change Reason: presentation till 6:00 + 1 hr travel S1: 7:30 − (still tight) S2: 8:00 → * but busy after 8 → book 8:15 (Miguel)

Rules that keep notes useful:
  • One or two words per idea — never a full sentence.
  • Always mark agreement and rejection: that is usually where the answer changes.
  • If you miss something, write "?" and keep going.
  • Use the same symbols every time you practise so they stay automatic.

Original Mini Example

Original practice

This is an original SelfPIP example, not a CELPIP recording. Read the situation, then answer the question — nothing is revealed until you choose.

Situation Two friends, Priya and Miguel, are on the phone about dinner on Saturday. Priya explains a timing problem, they discuss two options, and Miguel takes care of the booking.
Priya "I might be late on Saturday — my presentation runs until six and it takes about an hour to get across town."
Miguel "Should we move dinner from seven to seven-thirty?"
Priya "Could we say eight? I'd like to drop my bag at home first."
Miguel "That works, but the restaurant gets busy after eight. I'll book for eight-fifteen to be safe."
Priya "Perfect. Thanks for sorting that out."

Question — What do the speakers agree to do?

What to notice: the time changed twice before it was fixed. In Part 2, following the final version of the arrangement is usually what decides the answer.

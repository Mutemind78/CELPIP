Reading Part 4

Reading for Viewpoints

Read a text presenting viewpoints or arguments and identify positions, reasons, supporting evidence, tone, facts, opinions, and logical relationships.

Section 1 — What Is This Part? Official format: 10 questions SelfPIP study guide
Golden rule: Identify the viewpoint → collect the reasons → match the question to one piece of evidence

What is this part?

In Part 4 you read a text that takes a side on an issue — an opinion piece, an argument, or a description of competing viewpoints — and you answer questions about what the writer believes, why they believe it, what they acknowledge on the other side, and how they build their case.

The task tests argument tracking: following a position from claim to reason to evidence, and distinguishing facts from opinions along the way.

In everyday terms: you read an editorial or opinion article, then prove you understand which side the writer is on and what they use to support it.

Official format vs SelfPIP advice: Part 4 officially contains 10 questions based on texts presenting viewpoints. The strategies and examples on this page are SelfPIP study guidance, not official CELPIP rules.

What I Look For

Key things to track

Viewpoint / position

What side the writer is on, or which side a speaker takes. Look for markers like "in my view", "arguably", "I believe", or "critics say".

Reasons

Why the writer holds that position: logic, data, policy goals, practical benefits, or moral grounds.

Supporting evidence / examples

Statistics, expert names, real incidents, or concrete examples offered to prove each reason.

Facts vs opinions

Facts can be verified; opinions are beliefs or value judgements. The distinction matters when a question asks you to classify a statement.

Tone / attitude

The writer's stance toward the topic and sometimes toward the reader: approving, critical, neutral, alarmed, reassuring.

Logical relationships

How ideas connect: cause/effect, comparison, concession, or a conclusion drawn from the reasons.

Remember: "what the writer thinks" and "what you think" are two different things here. Always point back to the writer's own wording.
3

Strategy

Track an argument the way it is built, in order:

  1. 1
    Find the position.Identify which side the writer is on and mark it.
  2. 2
    Collect the reasons.List, in shorthand, each reason the writer gives for that position.
  3. 3
    Find the evidence/examples.Note the statistics, names, or incidents used to support each reason.
  4. 4
    Note concessions.Mark what the writer acknowledges on the other side, and how they respond to it.
  5. 5
    Match question to one piece of text.Choose the line that answers this specific question — not a different concession or example.
Why this matters: viewpoint questions test whether you can follow a writer's reasoning, not whether you agree with it. A position stated in the first paragraph and a concession in the middle are not the same thing.
4 SelfPIP guidance

Common Traps

🚨 Trap 1 — Confusing an example with the viewpoint

How it feels"This concrete example proves the writer's point, so it must be the viewpoint itself."

InsteadDistinguish the side the writer takes (viewpoint) from the evidence/examples used to support it.

🚨 Trap 2 — Choosing a true statement that does not answer the question

How it feels"The statement is true, so it matches the question and must be correct."

InsteadRe-read the stem carefully — a fact the passage mentions can still leave the asked-about viewpoint or reason untouched.

🚨 Trap 3 — Assuming the writer's opinion without textual support

How it feels"The writer implies this, so it counts as the viewpoint."

InsteadPoint to the sentence that states the writer's side; if it isn't supported, it isn't the answer.

🚨 Trap 4 — Missing contrast words

How it feels"The last claim before 'however' sounds like the writer's conclusion."

Instead"However"/"although" introduce a concession — the real conclusion usually follows, on the writer's own side.

🚨 Trap 5 — Ignoring qualifiers

How it feels"The statement seems right, so I pick it without checking the scope."

InsteadMatch qualifiers ("often", "some", "may", "generally") in the text to the scope of the option you choose.

Remember: these traps are SelfPIP study guidance, not official rules. Use them to review your own habits after practice.
5 Language that marks structure

Useful Signal Words

Signal words point to structure, not directly to answers. Use them to locate the part of the argument you need, then read carefully.

Contrast

however · although · on the other hand · nevertheless · while · despite · yet

Marks a turn or a concession — the writer acknowledges a different angle before returning to their own side.

Reason / cause

because · since · due to · as a result of · consequently · therefore · thus · that's why

Links a reason to the position, or a cause to an effect.

Opinion / viewpoint

in my view · I believe · I think · it seems · critics argue · supporters claim · arguably · from this perspective

Flags the writer's side of an issue.

Conclusion / result

therefore · as a result · in conclusion · to sum up · for these reasons · ultimately · so

Points to where the writer lands after laying out reasons.

Note: these signals are clues, not automatic answers. "However" introduces a concession — the real conclusion usually follows it, not precedes it.
6 Original SelfPIP example

Original Mini Practice

Situation A short opinion article about whether the town should build a new central library. Read the passage, then answer the questions. Answers are hidden until you choose.
Mini note-taking — try this for Part 4:
VP = viewpoint  |  R1/R2 = reason  |  E = example  |  CON = concession
While reading: scan for the first "VP" line, then tag each reason/example and any "however/although" concession. Keep meaning, not sentences.

Title: "Our Town's Next Library: Worth the Investment?"

Last year the town council proposed spending $3.2 million to replace the current Carnegie-era building, which leaks during heavy rain and can no longer hold the growing collection. I believe the new library is worth that cost, because it would provide modern study spaces, updated accessibility, and room for a local-history archive that the present facility cannot accommodate.

Critics of the plan point out that three neighbourhood branches are already within walking distance for most residents, and they argue we should simply renovate the existing main building instead. The critics are right that branches serve the east and west sides well; however, no branch offers the specialised archives that researchers need, and the architect's report shows renovation would cost about $2.8 million without solving the space problem for years to come.

Others still worry about the noise and traffic a construction project would bring. That concern is reasonable, but the council has already committed to limiting work to 7 a.m.–6 p.m. weekdays and to re-landscaping affected streets once construction ends. In the end, the long-term value of a full-service modern library outweighs a short-term inconvenience, so I recommend approving the new building at the next vote.

Q1. What is the writer's position on the new library?

Q2. Why does the writer mention the renovation cost of $2.8 million?

Q3. Which sentence is the writer's conclusion about the final decision?

Q4. A reader who says, "The writer admits critics are right that branches serve the east and west sides," is identifying:

Answer key and explanations

Q1 — B. The writer states "I believe the new library is worth that cost" and recommends approval. Options A, C and D describe other sides or details, not the writer's own position.

Q2 — A. The $2.8 million figure is given to weaken the concession that renovation is both cheaper and sufficient.

Q3 — B. "I recommend approving the new building at the next vote" is the writer's stated conclusion; the others are concessions or supporting facts.

Q4 — A. The writer acknowledges the critics' point about branch coverage before turning back to their own recommendation.

Habit for Part 4: when you see a concession, tag it mentally, then look for where the writer comes back to their own side.

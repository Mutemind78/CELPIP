Why Grammar Matters in CELPIP

The big picture

CELPIP raters read and listen to your ideas first. Grammar is how those ideas arrive. One repeated error — a missing article, a verb that does not agree with its subject — forces the rater to re-read or re-listen, and your clarity drops even when the idea itself was good.

You do not need perfect grammar. You need grammar that never gets in the way of your meaning.

Official format vs SelfPIP advice: CELPIP publishes general rating criteria for Writing and Speaking. The nine topics, examples and drills on this page are SelfPIP study guidance built from standard English grammar — they are not official CELPIP rules.
Golden rule: one idea, one clear sentence — if in doubt, make the sentence shorter.

Nine Core Grammar Topics

Open a card to study

A singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb. The trap is distance: phrases like "along with the manager" or "of the reports" hide the real subject from the verb.

IncorrectThe list of requirements are long.
CorrectThe list of requirements is long.
IncorrectEveryone have their own opinion.
CorrectEveryone has their own opinion.
Quick tip: cross out everything between the subject and the verb, then read just those two words aloud.

Time words choose the tense for you. "Yesterday / last week" demands the simple past; "since 2019 / so far" links the past to now, so it needs the present perfect (have/has + past participle).

IncorrectI am living here since 2019.
CorrectI have lived here since 2019.
IncorrectYesterday I go to the bank after work.
CorrectYesterday I went to the bank after work.
Quick tip: in one paragraph, do not jump timelines without a reason. Past events stay past until something brings you back to now.

Use a/an the first time you mention a singular countable thing. Use the when both speakers know exactly which one. Use no article for general plural or uncountable statements.

IncorrectI went to library after work.
CorrectI went to a library after work.
IncorrectShe is a honest supervisor.
CorrectShe is an honest supervisor.
Quick tip: a singular countable noun almost never stands alone — “I need an answer”, never “I need answer”.

Prepositions follow habit, not logic: you depend on, you are interested in, you arrive at a place in a city. Memorise the phrase, not the preposition alone.

IncorrectI arrived to work at eight.
CorrectI arrived at work at eight.
IncorrectWe discussed about the budget.
CorrectWe discussed the budget.
Quick tip: discuss, enter, reach and last take no preposition — “discuss the plan”, never “discuss about the plan”.

A pronoun must point to one clear noun — especially when you refer to a company, a group or a previous idea. After a verb or preposition (to, for, between, with) use me / us / them, not I / we / they.

IncorrectMy sister and me went to the pier.
CorrectMy sister and I went to the pier.
IncorrectSend the report to Lisa and I.
CorrectSend the report to Lisa and me.
Quick tip: remove the other person from the sentence — “me went to the pier” is obviously wrong, so it must be “I went”.

A complete sentence needs a subject and a verb. Two independent thoughts need a period, a semicolon, or a joining word such as so, but, because — never just a comma.

IncorrectBecause the bus was late. I missed the exam.
CorrectBecause the bus was late, I missed the exam.
IncorrectThe schedule changed, I stayed late.
CorrectThe schedule changed, so I stayed late.
Quick tip: read your sentence aloud — if you run out of breath before a stop, you have a run-on sentence.

Active puts the doer first: “The city closed the lane.” Passive puts the thing acted on first: “The lane was closed.” Use passive when the doer is unknown, obvious or less important — then keep the tense correct on be.

IncorrectThe report was finish yesterday.
CorrectThe report was finished yesterday.
IncorrectThe parcels are deliver every morning.
CorrectThe parcels are delivered every morning.
Quick tip: passive = be + past participle. Check the be-verb matches your tense (was / is / will be + delivered).

Zero: If you heat water, it boils. First (real future): If it rains, we will cancel the picnic. Second (unlikely now): If I had more time, I would volunteer. Third (past regret): If I had left earlier, I would have caught the bus.

IncorrectIf it will rain, we will cancel the picnic.
CorrectIf it rains, we will cancel the picnic.
IncorrectIf I would have more time, I would volunteer.
CorrectIf I had more time, I would volunteer.
Quick tip: never put will/would inside the if-clause — the if-clause takes plain present, plain past, or past perfect.

A modal is always followed by the bare infinitive: “You should check”, never “you should to check” and never “you should checks”. For requests, climb the ladder can → could → may as politeness rises.

IncorrectYou should to check the schedule first.
CorrectYou should check the schedule first.
IncorrectI must to arrive early tomorrow.
CorrectI must arrive early tomorrow.
Quick tip: must not = it is forbidden; do not have to = it is not necessary. Do not mix them up.
3

Three Sentence Types

Mix them to control your rhythm

Simple

One independent clause. The bus arrived late.

Use simple sentences for punchy points and clear recommendations — they are easy to follow under time pressure.

Compound

Two independent clauses joined by and, but, so, a semicolon, or a colon. The bus arrived late, so the meeting started without me.

Use compound sentences to show cause, contrast or sequence between two equal ideas.

Complex

One independent clause + one or more dependent clauses (because, although, when, if, since). Although the bus arrived late, the office waited for me.

Complex sentences show relationships between ideas — this is what “cohesion and coherence” rewards.

The reliable rhythm: open with a complex sentence to state the relationship, then prove it with short simple ones. Vary the pattern — a paragraph of only simple sentences looks like a list.

Use It in Your Practice

Cross-study

These nine topics only pay off inside real tasks. Take one rule from this page into each practice session and watch it show up in your scores.

  • Writing Task 2 (opinion): open with a complex sentence, prove it with short simple ones — and check subject–verb agreement before you submit.
  • Writing Task 3 & 4 (emails, reports): past simple for finished events, present perfect for news that is still relevant. Vary your connectors so paragraphs do not list.
  • Speaking Part 5 & 6: accuracy under pressure comes from the drills — say your sentences out loud, not just in your head.
  • Every answer: read the feedback on a wrong choice twice; the rule it explains is exactly what the rater is listening for.

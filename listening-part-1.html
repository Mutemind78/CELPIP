Listening Part 1

Listening to Problem Solving

Follow a conversation about a problem, understand the important details, track possible solutions, and identify the outcome.

Section 1 — What Is This Part? Official format: 8 questions SelfPIP study guide
Golden rule: Problem → Options → Discussion → Outcome

What is this part?

In Part 1 you listen to a conversation in which people work through a problem. Someone describes a difficulty, the speakers consider one or more options, and by the end there is usually a decision, a plan or a recommendation.

The task tests whether you can follow a conversation as it develops: what the problem is, which details matter, which options are suggested or turned down, and what the speakers finally decide. You are not expected to remember every sentence.

In everyday terms: you are listening to two people sort out a problem, and then answering questions about what happened.

Official format vs SelfPIP advice: Part 1 is the first of the six Listening tasks and officially contains 8 questions. The focus areas, strategies, signals, traps and examples on this page are SelfPIP study guidance — they are not official CELPIP rules.

What Should I Expect?

General expectations

Part 1 is a spoken conversation centred on one problem and the search for a solution. The audio itself is not published in advance, so treat the points below as general expectations, not a script.

  • One conversation, one main problem. The extract stays with the same situation instead of jumping between topics.
  • Speakers who react to each other. One person usually brings the problem; the other responds, suggests or questions.
  • Everyday, practical situations. Work, schedules, travel, services and similar daily issues are typical.
  • Natural spoken language. Contractions, short turns, hesitations and informal expressions are normal in conversation.
  • Options that may be rejected. A suggestion can be accepted, modified, or quietly dropped later.
  • Questions about the conversation as a whole. Several questions are usually answered across the extract, not by one isolated sentence.

The usual shape of a Part 1 conversation

Situation
↓
Problem
↓
Discussion and options
↓
Possible solutions
↓
Decision / recommendation
How to prepare: you cannot predict the exact words, but you can predict the shape. Recognising the shape is what makes the listening feel easier.

What Should I Listen For?

SelfPIP focus points

Keep these six targets in mind while the conversation plays. Together they cover what Part 1 questions usually ask about.

A

The problem

What is actually wrong?

Find the central difficulty, not a small side detail.

B

The people

Who is affected, and who is helping?

Notice who has the problem and who suggests ideas.

C

Important details

Which facts and limits matter?

Times, costs, distances, deadlines and reasons often decide the answer.

D

Suggested solutions

What options are put forward?

Label them in your head: option A, option B, option C.

E

Advantages and disadvantages

Why is each option accepted or rejected?

A reason against an option is often the reason the answer changes.

F

The outcome

What is decided, suggested or planned?

The end of the conversation usually carries the final direction.

Question-Type Strategy

SelfPIP guidance

Part 1 questions usually test one of these kinds of information. Expand a card to see how to handle it. These are common question purposes, not guaranteed question wording.

What it may ask

"What is the main problem?"

How to approach it

Choose the central issue, not a detail mentioned in passing.

What it may ask

"What happened?" / "What detail does the speaker mention?"

How to approach it

Attach the detail to the right person and the right part of the story.

What it may ask

"Why does the speaker mention...?" / "Why is this a problem?"

How to approach it

Listen for "because", "the reason is" and "that's why", and connect the reason to the right fact.

What it may ask

"What does the speaker suggest?"

How to approach it

Track every option, then check whether it was accepted or rejected afterwards.

What it may ask

"What do they decide to do?" / "What will happen next?"

How to approach it

Pay attention to the end of the conversation, where the direction is usually confirmed.

What it may ask

"What can we learn about...?" / "What does this tell us about the speaker?"

How to approach it

Stay close to what was said: choose what the conversation reasonably supports, not what you could imagine.

Remember: these are common question purposes, not guaranteed question wording, and a question type never guarantees an easy or difficult item.

Step-by-Step Strategy

SelfPIP guidance

Use the same three phases every time. The part-specific moves sit inside them.

1

Before listening

Quickly understand the situation and the question.

  • Read the question and decide what kind of information it wants.
  • Predict the likely shape: problem → options → discussion → outcome.
2

During listening

Track the important information as it appears.

  • Catch the problem in the first few turns.
  • Label each option as you hear it (A, B, C) and mark any rejection.
  • Listen for the change signal: "but", "actually", "the problem with that is".
3

After the relevant information

Compare the choices and choose the supported option.

  • Ask: does this answer the question, or does it just repeat a word?
  • Choose the option the conversation supports, then move on at once.

The four moves to remember

Catch the problem
↓
List the options
↓
Note what gets rejected, and why
↓
Confirm the outcome
"One missed question should not become three missed questions."

Common Traps

SelfPIP guidance

⚠ Trap 1 — Matching words instead of meaning

How it feels"I heard that word in the conversation, so this option must be right."

InsteadAsk what the speaker actually means. A repeated word can easily appear in a wrong option.

⚠ Trap 2 — Choosing the first idea mentioned

How it feels"The first suggestion sounds reasonable, so I'll answer with that."

InsteadKeep listening. In this task an early suggestion is often replaced by a later one.

⚠ Trap 3 — Confusing a detail with the main point

How it feels"This detail is definitely true, so it must be the main problem."

InsteadCheck what the whole conversation is about before you accept a detail as the main idea.

⚠ Trap 4 — Missing a change of opinion

How it feels"They agreed at the beginning, so they must still agree at the end."

InsteadStay alert for "but", "actually", "I'm not sure that works" — the position can shift in one turn.

⚠ Trap 5 — Getting stuck on one question

How it feels"I missed that one, so I'll replay it in my head until I'm sure."

InsteadLet it go and listen for the next answer. The audio does not wait.

⚠ Trap 6 — Overthinking a supported answer

How it feels"Maybe they meant something else, so the obvious choice is probably wrong."

InsteadIf the conversation directly supports an option, choose it. Do not add information that was never said.

Remember: these traps are SelfPIP study guidance, not official rules. They are best used as a checklist of your own habits when you review your practice.

Useful Signals

Recognition only

Problem

Language that introduces or describes an issue.

  • "the issue is..."
  • "I'm having trouble with..."
  • "we've been having a problem with..."

Suggestion

Language that proposes an action or an option.

  • "why don't we..."
  • "maybe you could..."
  • "how about..."
  • "one option would be..."

Reason

Language that explains why something happens.

  • "because..."
  • "the reason is..."
  • "that's why..."

Contrast

Language that changes direction or rejects an idea.

  • "however..."
  • "but..."
  • "actually..."
  • "the problem with that is..."

Agreement

Language that accepts an option.

  • "that sounds good"
  • "that could work"
  • "I think so too"

Conclusion

Language that shows the final decision or plan.

  • "so let's..."
  • "in the end..."
  • "I'll do that"
These expressions are examples of signals that often carry important information. They do not always mark the answer: the meaning of the whole turn decides that, not one single word.

Note-Taking Strategy

Small and fast

Do not write sentences and do not try to write every word — writing is slower than speech, so you will fall behind. Keep a handful of symbols that capture meaning, not grammar.

  • P problem
  • S solution / option
  • * agreement
  • − disagreement or rejected
  • → result or change
  • ? unclear

What a Part 1 note might look like

P: bus schedule changed → late for work S1: earlier bus − (arrives 1 hr early) S2: cycling − (no bike) S3: ask manager re start time → * possible ?

Rules that keep notes useful:
  • One or two words per idea — never a full sentence.
  • Always mark agreement and rejection: that is usually where the answer changes.
  • If you miss something, write "?" and keep going.
  • Use the same symbols every time you practise so they stay automatic.

Original Mini Example

Original practice

This is an original SelfPIP example, not a CELPIP recording. Read the situation, then answer the question — nothing is revealed until you choose.

Situation Two colleagues, Amir and Dana, are talking in a break room before their shift. Amir explains a problem with getting to work on time, Dana offers two ideas, and they agree on a plan.
Amir "I've been getting to work late ever since my bus schedule changed."
Dana "Could you take an earlier bus?"
Amir "I could, but the earlier one is almost an hour before my shift starts."
Dana "What about cycling? You said you live fairly close."
Amir "I'd like to, but I don't have a bike right now."
Dana "Then maybe ask your manager if you can start fifteen minutes later."
Amir "Actually, that might work. I'll talk to her tomorrow."

Question — What do the speakers decide to do?

What to notice: two options were rejected before the final plan appeared. In Part 1, following the rejections is often what separates the correct answer from the option that was only mentioned.

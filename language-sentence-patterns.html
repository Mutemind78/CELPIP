Patterns That Carry Your Answers

Why Patterns Matter

Mindset

CELPIP gives you seconds to plan a spoken answer and about twenty-five minutes to write one. In that time your brain must invent content, choose grammar, and organize the structure — doing all three from scratch is why answers trail off mid-sentence. A sentence pattern is a pre-built frame for the structure part: your attention stays on the idea, and the frame keeps the sentence moving.

The trap: a pattern with nothing inside it sounds memorized — and raters hear “In my opinion, …” hundreds of times a day. The frame earns zero points on its own; the specific detail you put inside it earns the score.

What a pattern actually is

  • A fixed frame + slots. “One of the main reasons is that ___.” The bold part never changes; the slot takes your content.
  • Five functions cover everything. Every CELPIP response is some mix of opinion, reason, example, result and comparison — this page gives you frames for exactly those five.
  • Grammar-safe by design. A pattern you keep intact is a pattern that can’t break: although + clause, despite + noun-ing, compared with + parallel items.
  • Two per function, not sixteen. Two solid frames per function (six total) beat sixteen half-remembered ones. Add more only when the first six run on autopilot.

How to practise: take one frame per day and say it out loud three times with three different contents (the 🔊 button gives you the rhythm first). By day six all six core frames are automatic.
2

The Pattern Bank

16 frames

Five functions, sixteen frames. Press 🔊 on a group to hear its rhythm, then read each frame out loud yourself — patterns are learned by the mouth, not the eyes.

Giving an opinion

Openers for your first sentence — or your conclusion.

  • In my opinion, ...
  • From my perspective, ...
  • I strongly believe that ...
Giving reasons

Turn a bare “because” into a full, scored reason.

  • One of the main reasons is that...
  • Another important factor is...
  • The main reason for this is...
Giving examples

One concrete example beats three abstract claims.

  • For example, ...
  • For instance, ...
  • A good example of this would be...
Explaining results

Show consequence: what happens next, soon and later.

  • As a result, ...
  • This would allow people to...
  • This could lead to...
  • In the long run, ...
Comparing

Speaking Part 6 and any “which is better” question lives here.

  • Compared with..., ...
  • On the other hand, ...
  • While X has some advantages, I believe Y would be better because...
Function → frame → content. Before every sentence, ask: “What is this sentence doing?” — stating an opinion, giving a reason, showing an example, explaining a result, or comparing. Grab that group’s frame, then fill the slot. Never pick the frame first and force content into it.
3

Use Them Well

Guidance
  • Vary your openers. Rotate the opinion frames — or drop the frame entirely and state your view directly (“Public transport is the better option because…”). Raters hear “In my opinion” all day; variety reads as natural English.
  • Fill, don’t recite. Pattern + specific detail = score; pattern alone = filler. After “For example,” name a real place, number or story — not another abstraction.
  • One function per sentence. The opinion sentence states, the reason sentence proves, the example sentence shows, the result sentence lands it. Don’t stack two opinion frames in one breath.
  • Protect the frame’s grammar. Swap the content, never the skeleton: although + clause / despite + noun-ing · the reason is that + clause · compared with + parallel items.
  • Speaking: skeleton, not script. In your ten prep seconds choose ONE frame and ONE detail. Memorized scripts sound robotic — and collapse the moment you forget a word.
  • Compare in parallel. “Compared with driving, taking the bus is cheaper” — gerund vs gerund, noun vs noun. Mismatched sides lose clarity points.
The 3-second rule: if you can’t finish the pattern’s slot with something specific within about three seconds, pick a shorter frame. A simple pattern filled well always beats an ambitious pattern left half-empty.

Use It in Your Practice

Cross-study

Patterns are the joint between language and task — pick the function first, then the frame, then pour in content. Take one frame into each session and notice where it lands.

  • Writing Task 2 (opinion essay): opinion frame in the introduction, a reason frame as your thesis, one “For example” per body paragraph, “In the long run” to close.
  • Emails, surveys and reports: a polite opinion opener, “The main reason for this is...” and one concrete example build every request and recommendation.
  • Speaking Part 1 (personal experience): tell the event as a result chain — “As a result, ...” — and pin it down with one example sentence carrying a time or place.
  • Speaking Parts 4 & 5 (opinion): opinion → reason → example → result inside sixty seconds is the whole task; Part 6 (compare) is the Comparing group, almost verbatim.
  • Daily habit: one frame each morning, said out loud with something real from your day — 🔊 first for the rhythm, then your own content.

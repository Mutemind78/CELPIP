Connectors That Make Ideas Flow

Link your ideas with the right transition every time, so your essays and speaking answers sound connected instead of like a row of separate sentences.

Why Connectors Matter

The big picture

A CELPIP answer is a chain of ideas. Connectors are the joints in that chain: they tell the rater whether your next sentence adds to the previous one, contradicts it, causes it, or gives an example. Without them, even good ideas can read like a list of unrelated statements.

  • They show logic, not decoration. A connector must describe the real relationship between the two ideas around it.
  • One connector, one job. Stacking two connectors in the same sentence usually confuses the relationship instead of clarifying it.
  • Vary them. Using only "and" or only "so" makes your answer feel repetitive; using five different contrast words in one paragraph feels forced.
  • Placement changes meaning. "However" at the start of a sentence is stronger and more formal than "but" in the middle.
Official format vs SelfPIP advice: CELPIP rates organisation and coherence in Writing and Speaking. The seven connector groups, patterns and exercises on this page are SelfPIP study guidance — they are not official CELPIP rules.
Golden rule: a connector earns its place only if the two ideas truly have that relationship.

The Connector Bank

7 groups · original examples

1 · Adding Information

Use when the next idea supports, extends or matches the previous one.

  • in addition — In addition, the library offers free evening classes.
  • moreover — Moreover, the plan costs the city almost nothing to start.
  • also / as well — The course is free; the materials are provided as well.
  • not only … but also — The station not only reduces traffic but also creates local jobs.
  • another point is that — Another point is that remote work cuts office costs.
In speaking: “What is more, the park is only five minutes from my apartment.” — a natural spoken version of “moreover”.

2 · Contrast & Concession

Use when the second idea pushes back against the first — or admits it before reversing.

  • however — The train is faster; however, it is considerably more expensive.
  • on the other hand — A car is convenient; on the other hand, parking downtown is difficult.
  • although / even though — Although the café is small, it is never crowded.
  • despite / in spite of — Despite the rain, the event continued as planned.
  • while / whereas — My brother prefers cycling, whereas I usually take the bus.
  • on the contrary — The course was not boring; on the contrary, I wanted it to be longer.
Watch out: “but” and “however” never travel together — “The train is faster, but however it is expensive” says the contrast twice.

3 · Cause & Reason

Use when you explain why something happened.

  • because — Because the bridge was closed, I took the train.
  • because of — Because of the closure, I took the train.
  • since / as — Since the store was closed, we went home.
  • due to / owing to — Due to the storm, the outdoor event was postponed.
  • the reason … is that — The reason I was late is that the bus broke down.
Watch out: because + full clause, because of + noun — and “the reason is because” repeats the same idea twice.

4 · Result & Effect

Use when you show what happened because of something.

  • therefore / thus — The road was flooded; therefore, we turned back.
  • as a result — The new schedule cut waiting times; as a result, complaints dropped.
  • consequently — Ice closed the runway; consequently, flights were cancelled.
  • so — It rained, so the game moved indoors.
  • this means that — Fares rose again; this means that commuters pay more each month.
Writing vs speaking: prefer “therefore / consequently” in writing; “so” sounds natural and fluent in speaking tasks.

5 · Giving Examples

Use to make an abstract claim concrete — one clear example beats three vague ones.

  • for example — Many residents bike to work; for example, my neighbour rides in all weather.
  • for instance — For instance, one delayed train can hold up thousands of commuters.
  • such as — Cities need basics such as clinics, schools and libraries.
  • to give you an example — To give you an example, my cousin learned English in six months by watching the daily news.
  • … is a good example of … — The new bike lane is a good example of low-cost urban planning.

6 · Sequence & Order

Use to give the rater a roadmap through your answer — essential in speaking long-turns and process descriptions.

  • first / first of all — First of all, check the timetable before you leave.
  • then / next — Then compare the prices of the two routes.
  • after that — After that, send the form to the office.
  • finally / lastly — Finally, keep a copy for your records.
  • meanwhile — I cooked dinner; meanwhile, my partner set the table.
In speaking: three steps with “first, then, finally” instantly sounds organised — even when the ideas themselves are simple.

7 · Conclusion & Summary

Use to signal that your answer is complete — the rater should never wonder if you have finished.

  • in conclusion — In conclusion, the benefits of the change outweigh the costs.
  • to sum up — To sum up, three small changes would improve the service.
  • overall — Overall, the new process is faster and simpler.
  • in short — In short, the plan is affordable and realistic.
  • that is why I think … (spoken) — That is why I think the city should keep the program.

Sentence Formation Patterns

Same idea · three safe shapes

Each pair below expresses contrast three different ways. The grammar changes with the connector — learn the pattern, not just the word.

However · separate sentence

S1. However, S2

The train is faster. However, it is more expensive.

Two complete sentences: period before “However”, comma after it. Never a comma alone between them.

Although · one sentence

Although + clause, S2

Although the train is faster, it is more expensive.

One sentence only. The although-clause can lead or follow — but never add “but” as well.

Despite · noun phrase

Despite / In spite of + noun or -ing, S2

Despite its higher price, the train is faster. · Despite being more expensive, the train is faster.

After “despite” comes a noun or an -ing verb — never a full clause with its own subject and tense.

Classic trap: “Despite of” and “although + noun phrase” are both wrong — it is despite (no of) or in spite of; although needs a full clause.

Build the Sentence

10 connector traps

Pick the connector that fits the grammar of the sentence — not just the meaning.

1. ___ it was raining, we went hiking as planned.

2. The bus was delayed. ___, we still arrived on time.

3. ___ the office stayed open late, we finished the report on time.

4. ___, the city should invest more in cycling infrastructure.

5. ___ you finish the first section, move on to the second.

6. The city offers many incentives, ___ free parking for new residents.

7. ___ the heavy snow, all flights were cancelled.

8. The company missed its targets; ___, three managers resigned.

9. ___ the price went up, demand stayed the same.

10. The east branch is not as busy ___ the west one.

5

Use It in Your Practice

Cross-study

Connectors are the cheapest way to raise the organisation marks of any answer — one well-placed word tells the rater how your ideas relate.

  • Writing Task 2: give each body paragraph one contrast connector (however / although) and one result connector (therefore / as a result) — no more.
  • Writing Task 3 & 4: link each request or recommendation to its reason with because or since, then close with a conclusion connector.
  • Speaking Part 5 & 6: natural spoken connectors (well, actually, on top of that) keep your 30-second answers flowing without memorised phrasing.
  • Before you submit: underline every connector and ask — does the relationship on both sides really match this word?

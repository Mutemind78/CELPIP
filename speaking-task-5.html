SelfPIP → Speaking → Task 5 — Comparing and Persuading

CELPIP Speaking · Task 5

Comparing and Persuading

Compare two choices, select a position, and persuade another person using relevant comparisons, reasons, and benefits.

60 s + 60 s preparation (two parts) 60 s response SelfPIP study guide
Golden rule: Commit to one option early, compare on what matters, and make your choice sound like the obviously smart one.
1 What this task is

What Is This Task?

In Task 5 two pictures or options are shown — for example, two holiday destinations, two apartments, two gifts — and someone asks you which one you prefer. You compare the options and persuade the other person to agree with your choice.

This task has two parts. In Part 1 you look at both options for 60 seconds without speaking. In Part 2 you get another 60 seconds to prepare, then 60 seconds to compare and persuade. Deciding your position during Part 1 means Part 2 preparation goes straight into arguments.

Part 1

60 seconds — view both options and lean toward one. No speaking.

Part 2 prep

60 seconds — lock the choice, pick 2 comparison points, note benefits.

Response

60 seconds — choose → compare → persuade, in that order.

Official format vs SelfPIP advice: The two-part structure and timings above are official CELPIP format information. The CHOOSE framework, language banks, traps, practice prompt, and model response on this page are SelfPIP study guidance — not official CELPIP material, and not a scoring rule.
2 How to answer

How to Answer This Task

The hub's CHOOSE framework turns the minute into a persuasion, not a description:

Choice → Options → How → Bonus
  1. Part 1 viewing (60 s): Compare both options and commit to one before this phase ends. Note the deciding criteria: cost, convenience, experience — whichever matters for THESE options.
  2. Part 2 prep (60 s): Write down: your choice in one phrase, two comparison points, one concrete benefit of your choice.
  3. Choice (≈10 s of speech): State your pick in the FIRST sentence — "I'd definitely go with the mountain cabin over the beach resort." The listener must know your side immediately.
  4. Options (≈20 s): Compare on your two criteria — acknowledge the other side's strength once (it makes you sound reasonable), then show why yours wins on what counts.
  5. How (≈15 s): Make it concrete — how your choice would actually play out: "We could spend mornings hiking and afternoons in town..."
  6. Bonus (≈15 s): One extra benefit plus a closing push: "...and it's cheaper, so we'd actually afford the trip. So all things considered, the cabin is the way to go."
Timing reality: The clock rewards decisiveness. If you're still weighing options at 30 seconds, you've already lost the persuasion.
3 Useful language

Useful Language

Stating your choice
  • I'd definitely go with... / I'd pick... hands down.
  • Without a doubt, my choice would be...
  • Between the two, I'd choose... every time.
Comparing the two
  • While both options have merit, ... is better because...
  • The main difference is that... whereas...
  • Option A may..., but on the other hand, Option B...
  • What tips the scale is...
Explaining how it works (How)
  • We could easily..., and then...
  • The way I see it working is...
  • All you'd need to do is...
Selling the benefit + closing (Bonus)
  • On top of that, ... / What's even better is...
  • You'd also get to...
  • That's why I'm convinced... is the better choice.
  • So all things considered, I'd say...
Comparison grammar

comparatives (cheaper, more convenient), -er vs. more, "whereas," and "not only... but also..."

Stress these words

your CHOICE and the winning criteria — "the CABIN is CHEAPER and more PEACEFUL."

4 Common traps

Common Traps

⚠ Trap 1 — Staying neutral

How it feels: "Both are good, it depends on your taste..." — you describe forever and never persuade anyone of anything.

Instead: Commit in sentence one. A wrong choice with strong arguments beats a fence-sit with perfect balance.

⚠ Trap 2 — Describing instead of comparing

How it feels: You list what option A has, then what option B has — two mini-reports, no head-to-head judgement.

Instead: Compare on shared criteria: "A is cheaper, whereas B is more convenient — and for a weekend trip, cost matters more."

⚠ Trap 3 — Burying the choice

How it feels: You reveal your pick in the final 10 seconds — the listener processed 50 seconds of argument without knowing your side.

Instead: "I'd go with X because..." in the first sentence — then argue. Everything after that reads as support.

⚠ Trap 4 — No benefit to the listener

How it feels: All criteria talk — price, size, distance — with no line about why that makes a better EXPERIENCE.

Instead: End with the payoff: "Which means we'd actually get to relax instead of budgeting every day." Sell the outcome.

5 Practice

Original Practice Prompt

Original SelfPIP prompt — not an official CELPIP question

Part 1 — view (60 s): You and your friend are planning a two-week trip to Canada. Option A is a city break in Toronto — museums, restaurants, nightlife. Option B is a cabin week in the Rockies — hiking, lakes, quiet nights. Your friend says, "You decide — which would you pick?"

Part 2 — prepare (60 s) → speak (60 s): Compare the two options and persuade your friend to agree with your choice. Explain how your choice would work in practice.

How to use this prompt: Force yourself to say your choice in the first sentence of the recording. Then check: did you compare on shared criteria, and did you finish by selling the benefit to your FRIEND, not just yourself?
6 Model response

Annotated Model Response

"I'd definitely go with the cabin week in the Rockies — that's the trip I'd choose without much hesitation.

Now, Toronto is genuinely appealing: the restaurants alone could fill three days, and everything is close together, so you'd waste no time on transport. But for a two-week trip, I think the cabin wins on the things that actually matter for a holiday. First, on cost — a cabin week is meaningfully cheaper, which means we'd fly, rent the car, AND still have money left for a nice dinner out. And on what the trip is FOR — we're both burned out from work — the Rockies deliver real quiet: lakes, trails, nights with no traffic noise.

Here's how it would work: we fly into Calgary on a Saturday, do one big grocery shop, and spend the week alternating between hiking days and lake days, with one day trip to a nearby town for supplies and coffee.

And honestly, the bonus is that we'd come back actually rested instead of needing a holiday from our holiday. So for two weeks, I'm convinced the cabin is the smarter pick."

Why this works

ChoiceSentence one states the side — the listener argues WITH the rest of the response, not against it.
OptionsAcknowledges Toronto's real strength once ("genuinely appealing"), then compares head-to-head on cost and trip purpose.
How"Here's how it would work: fly in Saturday, grocery shop..." — makes the choice concrete and executable, not abstract.
BonusEnds with the benefit to the FRIEND ("come back actually rested") + a closing push — persuasion, not description.
7 Self-check

Self-Check Before You Record

Task 5 — Comparing and Persuading

  • Did I state my choice in the first sentence?
  • Did I compare on shared criteria, not two separate lists?
  • Did I acknowledge the other option's strength once?
  • Did I explain HOW my choice would work in practice?
  • Did I finish by selling the benefit to the listener?
  • Did I sound decided from first second to last?
0 unchecked

Mindset

You're not submitting a balanced report — you're winning someone over. Decisiveness reads as confidence, and confidence is what gets persuasive responses over the line.

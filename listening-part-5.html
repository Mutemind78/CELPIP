Listening Part 5

Listening to a Discussion

Track multiple speakers, understand their ideas, recognize agreement and disagreement, and remember who said what.

Section 1 — What Is This Part? Official format: 8 questions SelfPIP study guide
Golden rule: Track the speaker AND their position.

What is this part?

In Part 5 you listen to a discussion between several speakers — a meeting, a class discussion or a group exchange — in which they share and compare ideas about one topic.

The task tests whether you can follow several voices at once: who said what, who agrees or disagrees, what reasons and examples they give, and how positions change.

In everyday terms: you listen to a group of people discussing one topic, and then answer questions about each person's view.

Official format vs SelfPIP advice: Part 5 officially contains 8 questions. The focus areas, strategies, signals, traps and examples on this page are SelfPIP study guidance — they are not official CELPIP rules.

What Should I Expect?

General expectations

Part 5 is a multi-speaker discussion rather than a single talk. The audio is not published in advance, so treat the points below as general expectations.

  • Several speakers, one topic. The discussion may touch several sub-points but stays on the same subject.
  • Turn-taking and interruption. Speakers react to each other, so ideas are added, questioned and revised as they go.
  • Positions matter. Each contributor may support, partly accept or reject an idea.
  • Reasons and examples. Views are usually supported with a reason or an example.
  • Some comments are side comments. Not every remark carries the main idea of the discussion.
  • Both positive and negative language appears. Agreement and disagreement are often expressed politely rather than bluntly.

How a discussion usually develops

Topic introduced
↓
Speakers give views
↓
Agreement and disagreement
↓
Reasons and examples
↓

How to prepare: practise noting one short tag per speaker — for example "S1 — cost", "S2 — time" — while you listen to any group conversation.

What Should I Listen For?

SelfPIP focus points

Keep these six targets in mind while the discussion plays. They cover what Part 5 questions usually ask about.

A

The topic

What are they discussing?

One topic, even if it is explored from several angles.

B

Who each speaker is

Which voice belongs to whom?

Fix the voices early — attribution questions are common here.

C

Each position

What does each speaker think?

Keep a one-word tag per speaker, for example "cost" or "time".

D

Reasons and examples

Why do they hold that view?

A reason or example often decides the answer.

E

Agreement and disagreement

Who accepts or rejects what?

Mark agreement and rejection; partial agreement counts as both.

F

Changes in position

Did anyone move from their first view?

Speakers often soften or revise a position later in the discussion.

Question-Type Strategy

SelfPIP guidance

Part 5 questions usually test one of these kinds of information. Expand a card to see how to handle it.

What it may ask

"Which speaker suggests...?" / "Who mentioned...?"

How to approach it

Answer from your speaker tags, not from the idea alone — the same idea may be mentioned by two people.

What it may ask

"What is the speaker's opinion about...?"

How to approach it

Hold one position per speaker and update it when they change direction.

What it may ask

"Which speaker agrees with the first speaker?" / "Which speaker disagrees?"

How to approach it

Listen for both halves of a sentence: "I agree, but..." is agreement plus a reservation.

What it may ask

"Why does the speaker hold this view?"

How to approach it

Link the reason to the correct speaker, then check it against the options.

What it may ask

"What does the speaker think by the end of the discussion?"

How to approach it

Follow the discussion to the end; the final position is often what the question asks about.

Remember: these are common question purposes, not guaranteed question wording, and a question type never guarantees an easy or difficult item.

Step-by-Step Strategy

SelfPIP guidance

Use the same three phases every time. In this part, labelling the speakers is the step that makes everything else possible.

1

Before listening

Prepare to separate the voices.

  • Note how many speakers there are and expect attribution questions.
  • Plan to give each speaker a short tag you can remember.
2

During listening

Track speakers and their positions side by side.

  • Label the speakers as they join (S1, S2, S3).
  • Write one tag per speaker, and mark agreement or rejection.
  • Use an arrow when a speaker changes or softens their position.
3

After the relevant information

Compare the choices and choose the supported option.

  • Check which speaker the question asks about before comparing ideas.
  • Prefer the option that matches what that speaker actually said.

The four moves to remember

Label the speakers
↓
Tag each position
↓
Mark agreement and disagreement
↓
Track any change of position
"Track the speaker AND their position."

Common Traps

SelfPIP guidance

⚠ Trap 1 — Mixing up which speaker said what

How it feels"Someone suggested the hotel, so it must be the first speaker."

InsteadAnswer from your speaker tags: two people can mention the same idea for different reasons.

⚠ Trap 2 — Assuming the group agrees with the first speaker

How it feels"The first speaker suggested it, so that is what they all decided."

InsteadFollow the responses. A first suggestion is often questioned or replaced.

⚠ Trap 3 — Reading partial agreement as full agreement

How it feels"They said they agreed, so the discussion is settled."

InsteadNotice the second half: "I agree with you, but..." adds a reservation that can decide the answer.

⚠ Trap 4 — Treating a side comment as the main issue

How it feels"Somebody mentioned parking, so parking is the topic."

InsteadWeigh how central a comment is: an aside is not the discussion's main point.

⚠ Trap 5 — Getting stuck after missing a speaker

How it feels"I lost track of who was talking, so the rest is hopeless."

InsteadRe-label the voices from the next turn onwards and keep collecting positions.

Remember: these traps are SelfPIP study guidance, not official rules. They are best used as a checklist of your own habits when you review your practice.

Useful Signals

Recognition only

Opinion

Language that introduces a personal view.

  • "I think..."
  • "in my view..."
  • "to me, the bigger issue is..."

Agreement

Language that supports another speaker.

  • "I agree"
  • "that's a fair point"
  • "exactly — that matches what I found"

Disagreement

Language that rejects or limits an idea.

  • "I'm not convinced"
  • "I see your point, but..."
  • "that wouldn't work for us"

Reason / example

Language that supports a position with detail.

  • "for example..."
  • "the figures suggest..."
  • "last year, for instance..."

Conclusion

Language that pulls the discussion together.

  • "so where does that leave us?"
  • "it sounds like we agree that..."
  • "let's go with..."
These expressions are examples of signals that often carry important information. They do not always mark the answer: the meaning of the whole turn decides that, not one single word.

Note-Taking Strategy

Small and fast

Do not write sentences. Keep one line per speaker: a tag, a position, and any change.

  • P problem / plan
  • S solution / option
  • * agreement
  • − disagreement or rejected
  • → result or change
  • ? unclear

What a Part 5 note might look like

Topic: team meeting venue S1 (Priya): office — $ = free S2 (Tom): hotel − (too expensive) S3 (Ana): library room → free + central S1: * library S2: − parking, but * if it saves budget → book library (2 wks ahead)

Rules that keep notes useful:
  • One or two words per idea — never a full sentence.
  • Always mark agreement and rejection: that is usually where the answer changes.
  • If you miss something, write "?" and keep going.
  • Use the same symbols every time you practise so they stay automatic.

Original Mini Example

Original practice

This is an original SelfPIP example, not a CELPIP recording. Read the short discussion, then answer the question — nothing is revealed until you choose.

Situation Three colleagues — Priya, Tom and Ana — are choosing a venue for their quarterly team meeting. Each of them puts forward a different idea.
Priya "For the quarterly meeting, why don't we just use our own office? It costs nothing."
Tom "The office is cramped with that many people. What about a hotel meeting room?"
Ana "I checked, and hotel rooms run about four hundred dollars a day. The library has a community room — free and central."
Priya "I had forgotten about that. I think the library works."
Tom "The only thing is parking there is difficult, but if it saves the budget, I am fine with it."
Ana "Then I will book the library room — they need two weeks' notice."

Question — Why does Tom have doubts about the library room?

What to notice: the question asked about one speaker's view, not the group's decision. In Part 5, attaching the right reason to the right speaker is usually the whole task.

Listening Part 3

Listening for Information

Listen for facts, details, explanations, names, numbers, locations, times, and other important information.

Section 1 — What Is This Part? Official format: 6 questions SelfPIP study guide
Golden rule: Listen for information, not just keywords.

What is this part?

In Part 3 you listen to someone giving information: a short talk, a presentation, an announcement or an informative interview.

The task tests whether you can collect facts and details from organised speech — the main topic, specific facts, names, numbers, dates, places and explanations.

In everyday terms: someone is explaining something to you, and you answer questions about the facts they gave.

Official format vs SelfPIP advice: Part 3 officially contains 6 questions. The focus areas, strategies, signals, traps and examples on this page are SelfPIP study guidance — they are not official CELPIP rules.

What Should I Expect?

General expectations

Part 3 is an informative piece of speech rather than a conversation. The audio is not published in advance, so treat the points below as general expectations.

  • One main speaker, usually. The information is organised, and the topic is normally stated early.
  • Facts and figures. Names, numbers, dates, times, places and quantities appear — and they matter.
  • Explanations. Speakers usually explain why something is the case, not only what it is.
  • Restated information. A detail is often repeated in different words, and the question may use a synonym.
  • Structure signals. Speakers organise a talk with "first", "another thing", "finally" and similar phrases.
  • Precision pays. Numbers and names are easy to mishear, so accuracy matters more in this part.

How the information is usually organised

Topic introduced
↓
First point
↓
Supporting detail
↓
Further points
↓
Summary
How to prepare: practise catching numbers, dates and names from short talks, and always note the unit (hours, dollars, kilometres) that belongs to a figure.

What Should I Listen For?

SelfPIP focus points

Keep these six targets in mind while the talk plays. They cover what Part 3 questions usually ask about.

A

The main topic

What is the talk about?

The introduction usually states it directly.

B

Key facts

What exactly is stated?

Facts are the building blocks of almost every question in this part.

C

Names and places

Who and where?

Names are easy to mishear; fix them as you go.

D

Numbers, dates and times

How much, when, how long?

Always attach the unit that goes with the figure.

E

Reasons and explanations

Why is this the case?

Explanations explain causes, choices and processes.

F

Paraphrases

How is the same idea said differently?

The option rarely repeats the exact words from the audio.

Question-Type Strategy

SelfPIP guidance

Part 3 questions usually test one of these kinds of information. Expand a card to see how to handle it.

What it may ask

"What is the main topic of the talk?"

How to approach it

Answer with the broad subject, not with one detail that was mentioned.

What it may ask

"What does the speaker say about...?"

How to approach it

Match the fact to the object it belongs to, not just to the topic.

What it may ask

"How long...?" / "When...?" / "How many...?"

How to approach it

Write the figure with its unit immediately; watch for words like "about" and "up to".

What it may ask

"Why is this the case?" / "What is the reason for...?"

How to approach it

Listen for "because", "the reason is" and "this is why", then link the reason to the right fact.

What it may ask

"Which statement matches what the speaker said?"

How to approach it

Compare meaning, not wording: the correct option often uses synonyms of the audio.

Remember: these are common question purposes, not guaranteed question wording, and a question type never guarantees an easy or difficult item.

Step-by-Step Strategy

SelfPIP guidance

Use the same three phases every time. In this part the note-taking habits do most of the work.

1

Before listening

Predict the kind of answer the question needs.

  • Decide whether the question wants a number, a place, a name or a reason.
  • Remind yourself that the option will probably paraphrase the audio.
2

During listening

Collect facts and figures as they appear.

  • Catch the topic in the introduction.
  • Note numbers with their units straight away.
  • Mark corrections: if the speaker restates something, the second version usually wins.
3

After the relevant information

Compare the choices and choose the supported option.

  • Check that the fact belongs to the right item.
  • Reject options that match only a word, not the meaning.

The four moves to remember

Catch the topic
↓
Note facts and figures
↓
Keep the unit with each number
↓
Restate the point in your own words
"Listen for information, not just keywords."

Common Traps

SelfPIP guidance

⚠ Trap 1 — Numbers attached to the wrong item

How it feels"The talk mentioned forty, so forty must be the answer."

InsteadCheck what the figure belongs to: items repaired, not visitors or volunteers.

⚠ Trap 2 — Matching words instead of meaning

How it feels"The option says Saturday, and I heard Saturday."

InsteadCheck the whole option — "every Saturday" and "the first Saturday of the month" are not the same.

⚠ Trap 3 — Confusing the topic with a detail

How it feels"Kettles were mentioned, so the talk is about kettles."

InsteadKettles are an example; the main topic is the repair café.

⚠ Trap 4 — Missing a correction or restatement

How it feels"They said nine o'clock first, so nine it is."

InsteadWhen a speaker restates a figure, the later version is usually the one they mean.

⚠ Trap 5 — Getting stuck on a missed figure

How it feels"I missed one number, so the remaining questions are lost."

InsteadLeave a gap, keep listening, and answer the questions you did catch.

Remember: these traps are SelfPIP study guidance, not official rules. They are best used as a checklist of your own habits when you review your practice.

Useful Signals

Recognition only

Topic

Language that announces the subject.

  • "today I'd like to talk about..."
  • "the focus of this talk is..."
  • "let me start with..."

Detail

Language that introduces a fact or an example.

  • "the first thing to note is..."
  • "one example is..."
  • "another point is..."

Number / measure

Language that qualifies a figure.

  • "about forty items"
  • "roughly two hours"
  • "up to twenty people"

Correction

Language that restates information.

  • "in other words..."
  • "sorry, I mean..."
  • "to put it another way..."

Summary

Language that pulls the talk together.

  • "to sum up..."
  • "so the key point is..."
  • "in short..."
These expressions are examples of signals that often carry important information. They do not always mark the answer: the meaning of the whole turn decides that, not one single word.
8

Note-Taking Strategy

Small and fast

Do not write sentences. Capture the facts — and keep each figure with the thing it belongs to.

  • P problem / plan
  • S solution / option
  • * agreement
  • − disagreement or rejected
  • → result or change
  • ? unclear

What a Part 3 note might look like

Topic: repair cafe (community centre) When: 1st Sat/month 10–2 − (not weekly) Bring: item + any missing part # repaired last month: 40 $: free (+ donations) → bring lamp or kettle

Rules that keep notes useful:
  • One or two words per idea — never a full sentence.
  • Always mark agreement and rejection: that is usually where the answer changes.
  • If you miss something, write "?" and keep going.
  • Use the same symbols every time you practise so they stay automatic.

Original Mini Example

Original practice

This is an original SelfPIP example, not a CELPIP recording. Read the short talk, then answer the question — nothing is revealed until you choose.

Situation A volunteer at a community centre gives a short talk about the repair café, where local people can bring small household items to be fixed.
You hear "Thanks for coming, everyone. Today I want to talk about the repair café at the community centre."
You hear "We run it on the first Saturday of every month, from ten in the morning until two. It is not every weekend — just that one Saturday."
You hear "Bring the item you want fixed, and if it is missing a part, bring that too, because we cannot order parts for you."
You hear "Last month our volunteers repaired forty items, and the café is still free — we only ask for a donation if you can."
You hear "So if you have a lamp or a kettle that has stopped working, bring it along on the first Saturday."

Question — When does the repair café take place?

What to notice: the correct option restates the audio in different words, while the tempting option repeats a word ("Saturday") with the wrong details attached.

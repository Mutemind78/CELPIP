Clear, Not Accentless

Mindset

CELPIP rates how easy your speech is to understand — not how Canadian, American or British you sound. This page is about listenability: the small mechanical choices (stress, endings, linking) that let a rater catch every word the first time. Your accent is welcome; confusing speech is not.

  • Finish your endings. "I work in a bank" and "I work in a ban" are one sound apart — plurals and past tense (-s, -ed) carry real information.
  • Stress the key word. The important word in the sentence gets the strongest, longest beat. Misplaced stress is the #1 reason raters miss meaning.
  • Keep a steady pace. Rushed speech loses endings and vowels; slow, flat speech loses energy. Aim for even rhythm with clear peaks.
  • Link words naturally. "want_to_go" style linking is normal English — fighting it makes you sound slower and choppier, not clearer.
You are never asked to imitate an accent. Nothing on this page asks you to sound like someone else. You are only adjusting which words get the emphasis so your meaning arrives intact.
Official format vs SelfPIP advice: CELPIP Speaking rates comprehensibility and fluency. The sound bank, stress tables, connected-speech patterns and record tools on this page are SelfPIP study guidance — they are not official CELPIP rules.
Golden rule: clear beats fancy — the rater should understand you the first time.

Word Stress

Noun vs verb pairs

Many two-syllable words move the strong beat when they change from noun to verb: beat 1 for the noun, beat 2 for the verb. Raters hear the beat before they hear the vowel, so the wrong beat makes the word unrecognisable even when every letter is right.

PairNoun — beat 1Verb — beat 2
recordRE-cord · “She kept a record.” re-CORD · “They recorded the call.”
presentPRE-sent · “He wrapped a present.” pre-SENT · “She will present the plan.”
objectOB-ject · “A glass object sat on the shelf.” ob-JECT · “The writer objects to the change.”
desertDES-ert · “They crossed the desert.” de-SERT · “The soldier left the unit.”
digestDI-gest · “The digest listed every article.” di-GEST · “It takes time to digest a heavy meal.”
conductCON-duct · “Their conduct at work improved.” con-DUCT · “The manager will conduct the interview.”
permitPER-mit · “You need a parking permit.” per-MIT · “The rules permit only two guests.”
Drill: say each row twice — noun beat first, then verb beat. Record yourself and check that beat 1 in the left column lands harder than beat 1 in the right column.

Sentence Stress

Content vs function words

In every sentence, a few words carry the message and the rest are glue. English rhythm = strong beat on the content words, quick and quiet on the glue. Raters follow your meaning through the beats, so flat “all words equal” speech is hard to follow even when every sound is perfect.

Content words — STRESS theseFunction words — swallow these
Nouns: report, manager, FridayArticles: a, an, the
Main verbs: finished, decided, needsPrepositions: at, in, of, to
Adjectives & adverbs: difficult, quicklyPronouns: it, him, some, this
Numbers & question words: two, where, whyAuxiliaries & conjunctions: is, have, and, but
Listen to the pattern: “I finished the REPORT on FRIDAY morning.” — “the, on, a” slide by; REPORT and FRIDAY land hard.
  • Stress the word that answers the question. If asked why, hit the reason; if asked where, hit the place.
  • Do not stress every content word equally — pick the one key word per idea and give it the longest beat.
  • Practice drill: before speaking, underline the 3–4 content words you will stress, then read the sentence twice.
A quick test: record yourself saying “It was VERY interesting” and “It was INteresting.” Different emphasis, same words — the second one sounds like you actually mean it.

Connected Speech

How English is actually said

In fast speech, words fuse, weaken and sometimes lose sounds. Native speakers do this automatically — and they expect to hear it. Recognising these patterns keeps listening comprehension smooth, and using them lightly in Speaking makes you sound fluent instead of word-by-word.

Written formSaid asExample
want to / want awanna“I wanna improve my speaking.”
going togonna“I’m gonna practise every evening.”
did youdidja“Didja finish the first task?”
could you / can youcouldja / canya“Canya say that again, please?”
kind of / a lot ofkinda / lotta“That’s kinda confusing.”
unstressed -ing-in’ (casual)“We were talkin’ about the schedule.”
give it to himgive-ti-m (merged)“Pass-ti-m the form, please.”
Speaking vs Writing: use these fusions when you speak and when you listen. In Writing Task 1–4, always use the full written forms — “gonna” in an email costs you marks.
Drill: listen to one short clip, write exactly what you hear (contractions and all), then read your transcript aloud while recording — match the rhythm, not just the words.

Hear → Repeat → Record

The 4-step pronunciation loop

Listening alone does not train your mouth. This loop closes the gap between what you hear and what you produce — it takes about two minutes per paragraph.

  1. HEAR — press 🔊 on the paragraph below (or any word card on this page) and listen once with your eyes closed.
  2. REPEAT — say it back immediately, copying the rhythm and the stressed words, not just the sounds.
  3. RECORD — press 🎙 and read the same paragraph into the recorder below.
  4. COMPARE — play your recording, then the model. Fix one difference (a stress beat or a missing ending) and read that sentence again.

Paragraph to read aloud

“Last month I moved to a new neighbourhood near the waterfront. On Saturday mornings the local market is bustling with shoppers, so I always arrive early while the streets are still peaceful. The vendors are reliable, the produce is fresh, and a friendly guard always directs traffic. It is a small routine, but it made a noticeable improvement in how comfortable I feel in this city.”

Do not chase perfection. One honest recording per day beats ten silent re-reads — the rater hears the speech you actually produce under time pressure, so practise producing it.

Use It in Your Practice

Cross-study

Pronunciation is trained in minutes, not hours — the trick is to attach it to practice you are already doing.

  • Speaking Part 3–6: before recording, underline the content words you will stress; after recording, check that you finished every -s and -ed ending.
  • Speaking Part 7 (advice): read your answer aloud once before submitting — the spoken pass exposes run-on sentences faster than re-reading.
  • Listening: when a clip blurs together, slow down your own speech patterns — knowing wanna/didja in your mouth makes your ear catch them too.
  • Daily loop: one paragraph, one recording, one fix — use the recorder above and keep yesterday’s version for comparison.
